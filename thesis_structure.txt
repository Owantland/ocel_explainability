1. Introduction
	* Explain Predictive Process Monitoring (PPM): predicting the outcome of an ongoing process before it finishes, so stakeholders can still influence the outcome. [van der Aalst et al. 2012]
	* Explain Object-Centric Event Logs (OCELs): representing process flows through the relationship between events and the multiple objects associated with each event step, as opposed to single-case-ID logs that force an artificial single-object view. [Adams et al. 2022]
	* Explain graphs and heterogeneous graphs, and their use for preserving OCEL shape and information without collapsing it into a single-case table.
		- Homogeneous representation: event nodes only, connected when they share a related object.
		- Heterogeneous representation: object nodes (with their own attributes) alongside event nodes, preserving more of the original process structure. [Zhai et al. 2025]
	* Explain how Graph Neural Networks leverage this graph shape to obtain more information for prediction than single-case-ID or purely event-sequence approaches, while noting this is contested in the OC-PPM literature, not a settled point:
		- Prior OC-PPM work has used non-GNN encodings successfully (Gradient Boosting on object-interaction features). [Galanti et al. 2023a]
		- A direct empirical comparison found GNNs underperforming Gradient Boosting on OC-PPM while costing 8-10x more training time, across 21 KPIs. [Galanti & de Leoni, n.d.]
		- This is precisely why this thesis's contribution is framed around explainability, not accuracy alone: a GNN that trades some accuracy for interpretability is only worth adopting if the interpretability is real and reliable.
	* Explain how Graph Neural Networks, although powerful, are normally treated as black boxes, which can hinder adoption in a business-process setting where a wrong or unexplained prediction has real operational consequences.
	* Explain the purpose of the thesis: providing an overview of explainability methods available for predictive process monitoring on heterogeneous graphs, noting the specific difficulty of finding implementations that actually support heterogeneous structures (most published GNN-explainability work targets homogeneous graphs). Explain the FOUR explainability components chosen for the project (corrected from an earlier three-method scope: fidelity metrics were added as a fourth, load-bearing component, not an afterthought):
		-- LOO - Perturbation analysis: assesses the significance of individual input features or components by systematically removing or altering them and observing the impact on the model's predictions.
		-- Feature attribution with Integrated Gradients: complements LOO with a finer-grained view of which raw features drive the important elements it highlights. [Sundararajan et al. 2017]
		-- Counterfactual explanation: demonstrates which changes to the factual input would be needed to achieve a different output prediction, answering an end-user question like "what would need to change for this process to finish sooner?" [Huang et al. 2022]
		-- Fidelity metrics: quantifies whether the explanations produced by the above are actually faithful to the model, not just plausible-looking. [Stevens et al. 2022]
	* Point forward to Related Work (below) rather than folding it in loosely, since the comparison baseline and the methodological template are both substantial enough to deserve their own chapter.

2. Related Work
	Object-centric predictive process monitoring
		* Prior work encoding object interactions as tabular features for KPI prediction with Gradient Boosting, evaluated on a real utility-provider log. [Galanti et al. 2023a]
		* A critical counterpoint: a direct comparison of LSTM/Gradient Boosting against GNN techniques across two processes and 21 KPIs, concluding GNNs underperform Gradient Boosting while costing substantially more to train. Directly motivates this thesis's explainability-first framing. [Galanti & de Leoni, n.d.]
		* A Shapley-value-based explainable decision-support framework for PPM (global + local explanations, validated with a 20-user study) — a feature-attribution-style precedent from outside the GNN literature worth contrasting with this thesis's Integrated-Gradients approach. [Galanti et al. 2023b]
		* Global predictive monitoring of object-centric processes: merging all ongoing process executions into one global graph via GNNs to capture interferences not explicit in shared objects, rather than treating each case in isolation. Referred to informally in project notes as "Pietro's work" — mentioned as a direction for future work in this thesis, since a single-trace/prefix view was chosen here for tractability. [de Leoni & Volpato]
		* An object-centric predictive process monitoring approach considering interactions between object types, evaluated on a public OCEL. [Gherissi et al. 2023]
		* An IoT-enhanced OCEL 2.0 extension, simulating a real-world logistics process with device-to-business-object interrelationships — relevant as an example of the OCEL 2.0 standard being extended for a specific domain, the way this thesis's own SQL-generalization work extends it for arbitrary datasets. [Wei et al. 2024]
	The direct comparison baseline
		* HOEG: a heterogeneous object-event graph encoding that does not aggregate object features, using a k-dimensional heterogeneous GNN for remaining-time prediction. This is this thesis's primary, structured comparison baseline — architecture, training methodology, and hyperparameter grid are all benchmarked against it directly (see Models, Training methodology). [Smit et al. 2024]
	The methodological template
		* A heterogeneous GNN framework combining post-hoc feature attribution and counterfactual explanation for a multi-modal, explainable land-use-inference task. This is the direct methodological precedent this thesis adapts: the HGT architecture pattern, the Integrated-Gradients-via-Captum attribution approach, and the four-component graph-dissimilarity metric used for counterfactual candidate selection. [Zhai et al. 2025]
	The GNN-explainability landscape
		* Broad taxonomic surveys situating this thesis's chosen methods (perturbation-based, gradient/attribution-based) within the wider space of GNN-explainability approaches (also including surrogate and generation-based methods not used here). [Yuan et al. 2022] [Nandan et al. 2025]
		* GNNExplainer: the first general, model-agnostic instance-level GNN explainer, formulating explanation as a learned soft mask over a compact subgraph and feature subset. Targets homogeneous graphs, but was still used in this thesis as a comparison point against LOO's node-importance ranking (see Models, Explainability layer design). [Ying et al. 2019]
		* PGExplainer: parameterizes mask generation across instances via a learned network rather than optimizing per-instance from scratch, improving generalization — cited as the natural lineage step between GNNExplainer and the heterogeneous explainers below. [Luo et al. 2020]
		* GNNInterpreter: a model-level (not instance-level) generative alternative, learning a probabilistic graph distribution that reveals a class's discriminative pattern rather than explaining single predictions — included to contrast instance-level choices (LOO, IG, counterfactual) against the model-level paradigm. [Wang & Shen 2023]
	Heterogeneous/temporal explainers considered but not adopted
		* HGExplainer: extends the GNNExplainer mask/mutual-information paradigm to heterogeneous graphs via meta-path sampling. Investigated for this thesis, but has no open-source implementation available, making it infeasible to apply directly. [Mika et al.]
		* HTGExplainer: extends the same mask/mutual-information paradigm to heterogeneous AND temporal GNNs. Investigated and confirmed to work correctly in its original DGL-based implementation, but a port to PyTorch Geometric (the framework this thesis's HGT model uses) broke the examples — attempted and abandoned, not simply skipped. Future work building the rest of the pipeline on DGL could revisit this. [Li et al. 2023]
	The counterfactual-explanation landscape
		* LORELEY: extends LORE with control-flow constraints for realistic counterfactuals in predictive business process monitoring — the method this thesis's counterfactual component is directly built on, adapted from a classification setting to a regression setting (see Models). [Huang et al. 2022]
		* NICE: a general nearest-unlike-neighbor counterfactual algorithm for heterogeneous tabular data, not graph- or process-specific — background context for the counterfactual-XAI landscape rather than a competing implementation. [Brughmans et al. 2022]
		* A dedicated survey of graph counterfactual-explanation methods, taxonomy, and evaluation metrics — more directly comparable to this thesis's graph setting than NICE, useful for framing evaluation-metric choices. [Prado-Romero et al. 2024]
	Grounding for perturbation-based interpretability (LOO)
		* General ablation-study methodology on conventional ANNs, investigating internal representation and robustness via structural removal — cited as conceptual grounding for ablation/perturbation as an interpretability technique, independent of graph structure. [Meyes et al. 2019]
		* "Representation erasure": an early, NLP-focused precursor to perturbation-based explanation, removing input dimensions/words/units to interpret model decisions — a direct conceptual ancestor of this thesis's LOO method. [Li, Monroe & Jurafsky 2017]

	Order Management dataset
		Synthetic OCEL 2.0 benchmark dataset; describe the process it represents and the KPI(s) chosen (PayOrder remaining time).
		Training recipe actually used: HGT with num_layers fixed at 2, matching HOEG's fixed message-passing depth. [Smit et al. 2024] This depth is sufficient here — every node type has a short enough path to the viewpoint.
	Logistics dataset
		Synthetic OCEL 2.0 benchmark dataset; describe the process it represents and the KPI(s) chosen (CustomerOrder-to-Depart remaining time), and that the viewpoint object was deliberately chosen to keep every node type reachable.
		Training recipe actually used, and why it differs from Order Management: this dataset's graphs require more hops to route information from all node types to the viewpoint. A fixed depth of 2 caused entire node types to be provably unreachable and, separately, caused the model to collapse to a near-constant predictor regardless of training budget; num_layers was instead tuned over {3, 4, 5}, with 3 found decisively best. This deeper-graph reasoning follows the same logic as global predictive monitoring's premise that OC-PPM needs the model to see more of the process, not less. [de Leoni & Volpato]
	Dataset processing
		Explain how SQL queries were used and generalized to take advantage of the OCEL 2.0 standard, so they could run against any standard OCEL 2.0 dataset, not just these two. [Wei et al. 2024] is a useful comparison point of another team extending OCEL 2.0 for their own domain (IoT-enhanced logistics).
		Explain the basic ideas behind OCEL generation:
			- How the OCEL is generated from the objects and events in the original dataset
			- How arcs between objects can be expressed directly (object-to-object) or inferred (event-to-event)
			- What attributes are chosen vs. generated to give the model more information
			- The choice to standardize both features and target to mitigate the effect of differently-scaled values
			- That the result is a series of graph prefixes, not a single global schema of all simultaneous events — future work could pursue the global view, per [de Leoni & Volpato]

4. Models
	Train/test split, ensuring temporal separation between train, validation, and test sets so no process in a later split begins before every process in an earlier split has ended (no information leakage from future events).
	Model architecture choice
		Why HGT over other heterogeneous architectures: contrast against HOEG's k-dimensional GNN, which does not aggregate object features the way this thesis's encoding does. [Smit et al. 2024]
		Why this specific explainability pipeline: adapting the combined HGT + post-hoc-explanation pattern from [Zhai et al. 2025] rather than building each component from scratch.
		Metric choice: MAE and RMSE, and why these are appropriate for this regression setting.
	The num_layers / weight_decay investigation
		A `weight_decay` hardcode mismatch (0.001 during hyperparameter sweep vs. 1e-5 as the regression default) was found to zero out several node types' input projections entirely during training — a training-side bug, confirmed via a controlled experiment varying only the optimizer, independent of architecture depth.
		Separately, num_layers=1 (freely selectable by the original sweep) made any node type without a direct edge to the viewpoint provably unreachable, regardless of training quality — a structural, not a training, limitation.
		Both were fixed: weight_decay corrected project-wide, and num_layers fixed at 2 for Order Management (matching HOEG) but tuned over {3, 4, 5} for Logistics specifically, since a fixed depth of 2 caused Logistics to collapse to a near-constant predictor no matter the training budget. This dataset-specific divergence is deliberate and documented, not an inconsistency — see Dataset, Logistics.
	Hyperparameter sweep
		A grid search over hidden_channels and learning rate (and, for Logistics, num_layers) was used to identify the most effective configuration per KPI, informed by priors from HOEG's own reported findings on which values tend to work best. [Smit et al. 2024]
	Training and validation
		Optimizer, loss function, and epoch/early-stopping budget: aligned to HOEG's single, unified 30-epoch/patience-4 budget for Order Management, restoring direct comparability; kept on a longer, pre-existing budget for Logistics specifically, since the unified budget was empirically found to undertrain its rarer last-event cases.
	Baselines for comparison
		Mean predictor, Gradient Boosted Trees on a tabular encoding, and a homogeneous GNN trained on a homogeneous representation of the same information — three of HOEG's four baseline categories. [Smit et al. 2024] Purpose: showcase whether the heterogeneous representation earns its added complexity, and report honestly where it does not (see Results).
		Report MAE against prefix depth, not just an aggregate figure, since the model should ideally become more precise the further along a process is.
	Explainability layer design (four components)
		LOO — Perturbation analysis: chosen as a model-agnostic method to identify the important elements of the graph by removing or altering them and observing the shift in prediction. Conceptually grounded in general ablation/erasure interpretability work. [Meyes et al. 2019] [Li, Monroe & Jurafsky 2017] Compared directly against GNNExplainer's node-importance ranking as a sanity check (see Results); GNNExplainer's own mask-based mechanism, and why it could be used here despite targeting homogeneous graphs by design, is described in Related Work. [Ying et al. 2019]
		Feature attribution with Integrated Gradients — chosen to complement LOO with a finer-grained view of which raw features drive the important elements it highlights, implemented via Captum. [Sundararajan et al. 2017]
		Counterfactual explanation — adapted from LORELEY, which was originally designed for a classification problem; adapting it to this thesis's regression setting required (a) a minimum-meaningful-difference threshold instead of a simple class filter, and (b) explicit prefix-depth-matching rules so candidates are compared at similar points in their process lifetime rather than across arbitrary lengths. [Huang et al. 2022] The four-part graph-dissimilarity metric used to rank candidates (feature/type/edge/structure) is adapted from the land-use template. [Zhai et al. 2025]
		Fidelity metrics — quantifies whether the above three actually reflect the model's real behavior, not just plausible-looking output, using a parsimony-and-faithfulness framework. [Stevens et al. 2022]
	Explainers considered but not implementable for this setup — see Related Work for the specific technical reason each was set aside: HGExplainer [Mika et al.], HTGExplainer [Li et al. 2023].

6. Conclusion
	Discuss the model's MAE/R² values — high or low, and why — grounded in the actual causes found during the project (the weight_decay bug, the num_layers reachability limit, and the more general finding that added model capacity can improve aggregate validation error while quietly degrading accuracy on the specific last-event cases this thesis cares about most).
	Discuss what the current explainability implementation can and cannot provide to an end user, and what more could be done.
		Correct an earlier framing here: GNNExplainer was not out of scope — it was implemented and used directly as a comparison point against LOO (see Results). What remains genuinely out of scope, and why, are the heterogeneous/temporal explainers HGExplainer and HTGExplainer, for the specific, concrete reasons given in Related Work (no open-source implementation; a failed DGL-to-PyTorch-Geometric port), not a general "not available" claim.
	State the real, current limitations honestly: some node types' feature attribution has not been fully root-caused even after the reachability fix; the Logistics dataset had at least one node type that remained structurally disconnected from the viewpoint under any tested depth; and the binary-classification KPI path was deliberately left unfinished as out of scope for this thesis.
	Future work: a global, cross-trace view of the process rather than per-prefix isolation, following [de Leoni & Volpato]; a k-dimensional GNN baseline to compare directly against HOEG's own published architecture rather than only its reported numbers [Smit et al. 2024]; and revisiting HTGExplainer if a DGL-based pipeline is ever built [Li et al. 2023].
